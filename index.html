Practical English 5 · Inviting and offering · Level A1–A2 · 90 minutes

In this lesson, you’ll learn how to invite people and how to answer invitations — to a party, the cinema or a concert. You’ll practise the polite phrases would you like + noun and would you like + to + verb, watch two short videos, and hear real invitations.

Vocabulary for this lesson

Click any word or phrase to see its card: picture, transcription, audio, definition and translation.

Would you like…?

We use would you like to offer something or to invite someone. It is polite and friendly.

1 · Offer: Would you like + noun?
  • Would you like a cup of coffee?
  • Would you like some help with the invitations?
2 · Invitation: Would you like + to + verb?
  • Would you like to come to my party on Saturday?
  • Would you like to go to the cinema on Friday?
AnswerPositive ✓Negative ✗ (polite)
Offer
(Would you like a coffee?)		 Yes, please! No, thank you. I’m fine.
Invitation
(Would you like to come?)		 I’d love to!
That sounds great!		 I’d love to, but I’m busy.
Maybe another time.
💡 Remember: I’d = I would. After would you like we use to + verb — never “Would you like come…”. To say politely what you want, use I’d like: “I’d like a coffee, please.” With close friends, “Do you want…?” is also OK, but “Would you like…?” is more polite.

Language focus~8 minutes

Inviting and offering with “would you like”

You saw Would you like…? and I’d like in the video. Now listen, look and repeat. Click 🔊 to hear each example in British English.

1 · Offering something 🫖

Would you like + noun?
Would you like a cup of coffee?
Would you like some help with the invitations?

Answer: Yes, please! · No, thank you. I’m fine.

2 · Inviting someone 🎟️

Would you like + to + verb?
Would you like to come to my party?
Would you like to go to the cinema?

Answer: I’d love to! · I’d love to, but I’m busy.

Answering an invitation

✓ Accepting✗ Declining politely
I’d love to!
That sounds great!
Yes, please!
I’d love to, but I’m busy. Sorry!
Sorry, I can’t.
Maybe another time.
💡 I’d = I would. I’d like = a polite way to say what you want: “I’d like a coffee, please.” When we decline politely, we usually say something positive first: “I’d love to, but…” — this is friendly in British culture.

Video script — “Meeting an old friend”

Steve: Hi, Jenny. What a surprise! — Jenny: Hi, Steve! — Steve: How are you? You’re looking great. — Jenny: Thanks. I’m well. How about you? — Steve: I’m OK. Hey, it’s starting to rain. Would you like a coffee? — Jenny: Oh, thanks, Steve, but I have a meeting in an hour. — Steve: Oh, come on, Jenny. I’d like to talk to you. — Jenny: OK. A quick coffee. — Steve: Jenny, I’d like to ask you something. — Jenny: Yes? — Steve: There’s an exhibition of Picasso at the MOMA next week. I know you love Picasso. Would you like to come with me? — Jenny: Listen, Steve. I’m really busy at work at the moment. Next week isn’t a good week for me. — Steve: Sure. No problem. Maybe we can meet one evening, when you have more time and… have dinner or see a show? — Jenny: Listen, Steve. I don’t think it’s a good idea. But thanks. Oh, look at the time! I need to go. Let’s ask for the check.

Anticipated difficulties & correction points

  • Rhythm: students give every word equal weight — drill the 9.16 build-up line by line; clap or tap the strong words.
  • “Would you like come…” (dropping to) — correct immediately; recycle in Screen 12 review.
  • “I’d like” vs “Would you like” — request vs invitation; use Screen 10 phrases as examples.
  • Declining politely: “I don’t think it’s a good idea” — discuss tone (firm but friendly).
  • Times: “half past three”, “8.00” — check pronunciation /hɑːf pɑːst/.
